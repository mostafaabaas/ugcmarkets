Guide 1 · The flow

How UGC marketplaces work: from brief to approved delivery

A UGC marketplace turns hiring a content provider into one tracked flow instead of a dozen disconnected chats. Instead of finding a videographer in one app, negotiating in a second and sending money on trust in a third, every step of the deal — the brief, the offers, the payment, the delivery and the approval — happens in one place, with a record. Here is the flow, stage by stage:

1 · The brand posts one brief

The brand describes the job once: the service needed, the deadline, the budget and reference examples. That single brief is visible to every provider in the matching category — the brand does not message providers one by one and re-explain the job each time.

2 · Providers answer with offers

Providers whose craft matches respond with offers — their price and their terms. The brand compares them side by side and checks each provider's public profile and portfolio before choosing; providers who have completed identity verification carry a verified badge on their profile.

3 · The client funds escrow

When the brand accepts an offer, it funds the platform wallet. Only then does work start: the provider never works on a promise, and the brand's money is held by the platform rather than handed straight over (Guide 2 explains the mechanics).

4 · The order runs in a deal chat

Accepting an offer opens a deal chat for that order: the brief, the files, the drafts and the revision requests all live in one thread, overseen by the platform team — nothing depends on scrolling back through a personal chat app.

5 · Delivery, approval, release

The provider delivers inside the chat. The brand reviews the work, requests fixes if something is off, and approves when the delivery matches the brief — and that approval is what releases the payment from escrow to the provider.

The structure exists to kill the two failures that plague hiring creative work in open chats: paying for work that never arrives, and delivering work that never gets paid for. A marketplace does not remove the need to choose well — it removes the need to trust blindly.

Guide 2 · Payments

Escrow payments for creative work, explained

Escrow means a neutral party — the platform — holds the client's money between payment and delivery, and releases it only when the client approves the work. It is the oldest trust mechanism in commerce, applied to the way creative work is actually bought. Here is how it runs on the influUGC marketplace:

How the money moves

  • Funded before work starts. When a deal is accepted, the client funds the platform wallet and the deal amount is held. The provider can see the job is funded before picking up a camera — no one starts on a promise.
  • Held during production. While the provider works, the money sits with the platform. It is not in the provider's account, so the brand is not exposed to undelivered work — and it is no longer just sitting in the brand's account either, so the provider is not exposed to a client who goes quiet.
  • Delivered and reviewed in the deal chat. Drafts and finals arrive in the order's chat, and revision requests happen there too, against the original brief.
  • Released on approval. The release is the client's act: approving the delivery moves the payment to the provider. Until then it stays held — approval, not mere delivery, is the trigger.

Why it protects both sides

For the brand, escrow means never paying for work you did not receive: your checkpoint is the approval step, and the money does not move before it. For the provider, it means never producing unfunded work and never chasing invoices afterwards — the payment is already there, waiting on the client's approval of a finished job.

What escrow does not do

Escrow does not judge whether a video is good — that judgment is the brand's, exercised at the approval step, which is exactly why a clear brief matters (Guide 3). What escrow replaces is trust in a stranger: both sides rely on the process instead of on each other's promises. The marketplace's terms are shared at onboarding, before any deal starts.

Guide 3 · Briefing

How to write a creator brief that gets good offers

Providers can only price what they can understand. A specific brief gets more offers, faster, at honest prices. A vague one gets silence — or padded quotes from providers pricing in the risk of not knowing what you want. Whether you brief a creator, a videographer or an editor, the same elements decide the response:

What a good brief includes

  • The deliverable, exactly — "one 60-second vertical product video" or "a half-day shoot, 30 edited photos". A countable thing, not "some content".
  • The product and who it is for — one or two lines. Providers price effort, and effort follows clarity about what is being sold and to whom.
  • Format and specs — vertical or horizontal, edited files or raw footage too, captions, the language of any voiceover.
  • A real deadline — an actual date, and whether it is fixed (a launch, an event) or flexible. Providers plan their week around deadlines; a brief without one reads as not-yet-real.
  • References — two or three links to work in the style you want. "Like this" settles questions that three paragraphs cannot.
  • Usage, stated upfront — where the finished work will run: organic posts, a product page, paid ads. Usage changes the price, and surprises after delivery are where deals break down.
  • The budget, honestly — a range or a ceiling. Providers self-select against it. Hiding the budget does not make offers cheaper; it makes serious providers skip the brief.

The mistakes that get a brief ignored

"Price?" with no deliverable — there is nothing to price. Asking for finished work as a free "test" before any funded deal. And changing the deliverable mid-order instead of agreeing revisions against the original brief in the deal chat. Each one tells providers the job is not serious yet.
